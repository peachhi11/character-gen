# Relationship Wounds, Attachment, Modes, and Power Dynamics

## Purpose
This reference distills the strongest CharacterGen-usable material from:
- the schema therapy model pages
- early maladaptive schema summaries
- introductory attachment-style material
- mode journalling and mode-management material
- the 2022 D/s narrative-language paper

Use it to turn relationship psychology into:
- visible behavior
- conflict style
- support preference
- trust pattern
- intimacy pattern
- scene pressure

Do not use it as:
- a clinical diagnosis system
- proof that every person in a role behaves the same way
- permission to flatten characters into therapy labels

## Non-Verbatim Rule
This file is a behavior-translation reference, not output copy.

Use it to infer:
- triggers
- support needs
- conflict habits
- intimacy friction

Do not print therapy-shaped wording back into the final output unless the character would realistically think or speak that way.

## Core Rule
The useful unit is not the label.
The useful unit is:
- wound
- trigger
- coping mode
- attachment reaction
- behavior

If a framework does not help predict what the character does when they feel unsafe, it is dead weight.

## Core Unmet Needs
Many useful relationship patterns can be traced back to unmet needs around:
- safety
- stable attachment
- love and nurturance
- empathy and validation
- guidance and realistic limits
- belonging
- autonomy
- play and joy

Characters become more legible when their strongest unmet need explains what they keep chasing, fearing, or misreading in love.

## Attachment Under Stress
Attachment matters most when closeness, distance, conflict, shame, or dependence activates threat.

### Secure
Usually able to:
- ask for reassurance directly
- tolerate space without spiraling
- repair after conflict
- stay emotionally present without forcing

Useful translation:
- clear bids for closeness
- steadier pacing
- less distortion under pressure
- can be vulnerable without collapsing into panic or contempt

### Anxious
Usually fears:
- abandonment
- rejection
- not mattering enough

Common behavior:
- reassurance seeking
- rumination
- over-reading signals
- protest behavior
- clinging, overexplaining, or escalating when connection feels shaky

Support preference:
- visible reassurance
- explicit verbal commitment
- consistency after rupture

Failure mode:
- pushes for closeness so hard that they strain the bond they are trying to secure

### Avoidant
Usually fears:
- vulnerability
- dependence
- exposure
- being controlled by need

Common behavior:
- minimizing feelings
- dismissing emotional conversations
- framing distance as independence or rationality
- withdrawing when intimacy becomes real
- anger or contempt used to shut down emotional demands

Support preference:
- calm, non-invasive steadiness
- practical help
- room to re-approach voluntarily

Failure mode:
- protects against rejection so aggressively that they create loneliness and resentment

### Disorganized
Usually holds both:
- longing for closeness
- fear of what closeness will do

Common behavior:
- cling then bolt
- mixed signals
- mode-flipping between panic and numbness
- self-sabotage to regain control
- suspiciousness, shutdown, or lashing out when overwhelmed

Support preference:
- consistency
- low-chaos reassurance
- predictability
- repair that does not feel coercive

Failure mode:
- creates the instability they fear in order to end uncertainty on their own terms

## Schema Clusters That Matter Most for Relationships

### Abandonment / Instability
Core expectation:
- people leave
- support is unreliable

Likely behavior:
- hypervigilance for distance
- panic at inconsistency
- overreaction to lateness, mood shifts, or ambiguity

### Emotional Deprivation
Core expectation:
- no one will really meet my emotional needs

Likely behavior:
- asking indirectly
- settling for crumbs
- numb self-sufficiency
- intense reaction when genuine care finally appears

### Mistrust / Abuse
Core expectation:
- closeness leads to harm, manipulation, humiliation, or exploitation

Likely behavior:
- testing
- suspicion
- defensive aggression
- difficulty accepting softness at face value

### Defectiveness / Shame
Core expectation:
- if I am seen clearly, I will be rejected

Likely behavior:
- hiding
- apologizing for existing
- sexual or emotional inhibition
- flinching from praise
- attraction mixed with exposure panic

### Social Isolation / Alienation
Core expectation:
- I am outside normal belonging

Likely behavior:
- awkward observation from the edges
- over-attachment to the few who do make room
- using niche worlds or private rituals as belonging substitutes

### Dependence / Incompetence
Core expectation:
- I cannot manage alone

Likely behavior:
- helplessness
- overreliance
- handing off decisions under stress
- panic when not externally guided

### Enmeshment / Undeveloped Self
Core expectation:
- separation feels like loss of self or betrayal

Likely behavior:
- blurred boundaries
- difficulty knowing what they want apart from the relationship
- strong guilt around individuation

### Subjugation
Core expectation:
- my needs and anger are unsafe, invalid, or too costly

Likely behavior:
- yielding too quickly
- suppressing preferences
- compliance followed by resentment
- hypersensitivity to feeling trapped

### Self-Sacrifice
Core expectation:
- care means giving more than I receive

Likely behavior:
- chronic overgiving
- difficulty asking
- exhaustion disguised as devotion
- resentment that appears late and sideways

### Approval-Seeking
Core expectation:
- worth depends on being wanted, admired, or chosen

Likely behavior:
- image management
- craving response cues
- inauthentic choices to preserve desirability or acceptance

### Emotional Constriction / Inhibition
Core expectation:
- spontaneity, vulnerability, affection, or intensity will cost me

Likely behavior:
- stoicism
- over-rationality
- awkwardness around tenderness
- difficulty expressing both positive and negative feeling

### Unrelenting Standards / Punitiveness
Core expectation:
- mistakes make me unsafe, weak, or unworthy

Likely behavior:
- perfectionism
- harsh self-correction
- impatience with weakness
- difficulty relaxing into intimacy

## Mode Logic
Modes are the fast-moving, scene-level states a character drops into when a wound is activated.

Most useful broad categories:
- vulnerable child
- angry child
- impulsive child
- demanding critic
- punitive critic
- coping protector modes
- healthy adult

### Vulnerable Child
Shows up as:
- fear
- loneliness
- shame
- helplessness
- feeling small, unwanted, or overwhelmed

### Angry Child
Shows up as:
- outrage that needs are not being met
- protest
- lashing out
- impatience

### Demanding Critic
Shows up as:
- pressure
- impossible standards
- "should" language
- no rest, no softness, no mistakes

### Punitive Critic
Shows up as:
- self-blame
- inner cruelty
- belief that pain or punishment is deserved

### Coping Modes
Shows up as:
- detachment
- overcontrol
- people-pleasing
- bullying
- disappearing
- intellectualizing
- distracting

### Healthy Adult
Shows up as:
- naming what is happening
- pausing before reacting
- seeking reality over panic
- meeting needs cleanly
- holding both boundaries and care

## Mode Activation Chain
The most useful translation from the journalling material is:
1. situation
2. thoughts / story
3. feelings
4. physical sensations
5. urges
6. behavior

This is one of the best ways to convert psychology into playable CharacterGen logic.

Example use:
- trigger: delayed text reply
- story: I am being abandoned / I said too much / they are pulling away
- feeling: fear, shame, anger
- body: tight chest, nausea, buzzing, numbness
- urge: chase, withdraw, pick a fight, go cold
- behavior: spam texting, silent treatment, brittle casualness, impulsive cutoff

## Power Dynamics and D/s Role Logic
Role should be treated as a relationship stance, not just an activity list.

### Dominant-coded stance
The strongest grounded signal is:
- other-focus
- ownership of responsibility
- scene stewardship
- monitoring comfort and affect
- directing structure rather than merely exerting force

Useful translation:
- tracks the other person's state
- frames choices, pacing, and safety
- notices details
- carries responsibility as part of the role
- may express authority through calm direction rather than volume

Common mistake to avoid:
- reducing Dominant behavior to bossiness, cruelty, or generic arrogance

### Submissive-coded stance
The strongest grounded signal is:
- self-experience focus
- power-awareness
- sensation-awareness
- response to direction, containment, or surrender

Useful translation:
- notices what is being done, felt, permitted, or withheld
- tracks shifts in control and safety
- may become intensely aware of body, affect, and permission
- often reveals power dynamics through language about being guided, held, made, allowed, trusted, or yielded

Common mistake to avoid:
- reducing submissive behavior to weakness, childishness, or lack of agency

### Core distinction
Dominant-coded behavior often expresses:
- responsibility
- leadership
- partner-monitoring
- outwardly directed attention

Submissive-coded behavior often expresses:
- surrender
- receptivity
- sensation focus
- inward and power-aware attention

Neither stance should automatically imply:
- pathology
- moral superiority
- emotional maturity
- fixed bedroom-to-life transfer

## Useful Crossovers
Some patterns combine especially well:

### Anxious + submissive-coded
- highly reassurance-sensitive
- may read structure, praise, and containment as safety
- vulnerable to overgiving or over-yielding when afraid to lose connection

### Avoidant + dominant-coded
- may prefer control to vulnerability
- can use structure and authority to stay engaged without feeling exposed
- risks becoming rigid, overly detached, or contemptuous under emotional pressure

### Shame + submissive-coded
- may crave surrender, permission, softness, praise, or gentle correction
- can also spiral into self-erasure or deserved-punishment logic if not grounded

### Unrelenting standards + dominant-coded
- may become hyper-responsible, exacting, and controlled
- risks confusing stewardship with perfectionistic pressure

These are not rules.
They are useful interaction hypotheses when aligned with the character's broader structure.

## Support and Repair Preferences
Different psychologies need different kinds of repair.

### Often helps anxious characters
- explicit reassurance
- naming the bond directly
- predictable follow-through

### Often helps avoidant characters
- calm tone
- reduced pursuit pressure
- practical steadiness
- room to re-enter without humiliation

### Often helps shame-heavy characters
- non-mocking warmth
- specific praise
- reassurance that they are not disgusting, burdensome, or too much

### Often helps subjugated or self-sacrificing characters
- invitations to choose
- explicit permission to want
- not punishing anger or difference

### Often helps disorganized characters
- consistency
- slow pacing
- not escalating every rupture into catastrophe
- proof that closeness does not erase autonomy

## What to Avoid
- treating attachment style as a fixed personality
- treating D/s role as a full identity shortcut
- forcing every wound into childhood exposition
- importing therapy language directly into dialogue
- assuming submission means innocence or dominance means hardness
- confusing intensity with depth

## CharacterGen Takeaway
The best use of this material is to infer:
- what the character believes love will cost
- what kind of closeness activates fear
- which story turns on when they feel unsafe
- which mode takes over next
- what their body urges them to do
- what kind of repair they can and cannot accept

That is what makes psychology playable.
